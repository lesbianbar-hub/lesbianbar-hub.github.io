Gender

"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" (De Beauvoir, p.234)

In their 1949 work The Second Sex, Simone de Beauvoir constructs gender as a performance that exists within the realm of the society that we have built. The famous quote describing the act of becoming a woman is the opening statement of their chapter Childhood. De Beauvoir expresses what has become known as the sex-gender distinction, the description of the difference between biological sex and what we perceive as the societally correct connotation of a gender. In de Beauvoir’s work, they argue that women have been made the second sex, the Other, in society, by men mystifying them. With men arguing to not understand women’s problems and therefore being unable to help them, a hierarchy is created. Femininity has been rendered the inferior by the historical and social construction that compares it to masculinity, and women are therefore oppressed. This hierarchy places men at the top, and women, the mystified other sex, below them. De Beauvoir’s work is fundamental to feminist discourse as well as gender studies, and has been built upon over the years. Many concepts have been introduced over the years, building on this construction of gender, of which the most influential ones will be introduced in this section. Butler builds on De Beauvoir’s foundation that gender is not fixed, but rather socially constructed, and states in their famous work ”Gender trouble” that the practices we engage with in a society are a performance of gender which through performance reinforces the way a society defines gender. The gender that a person is perceived as therefore comes with an expectation of what their identity consists of. Specifically, Butler points out that the gender norms we live with are reinforced through repetition, and the norms we reproduce do not give us agency outside of the constructions in which we repeat them. We are expected to perform gender in a particular way, and through the reproduction, these norms are reinforced within the society. Heritage explains the historical development of the distinction between sex, the biological traits that associate to either male or female, and gender. They state that the two concepts have been seen as synonymous historically, with the sex that an infant is being assigned with at birth due to their reproductive organs matching inherently with their gender. Depending on the perceived gender, behaviors are encouraged or discouraged in individuals to match the societies’ expectation of being ’male’ or ’female’. Pryzgoda and Chrisler define gender as the social, behavioral, and psychological characteristics of humans. Heritage further explains that the proposal of sex being biologically determined while gender is socially constructed, has by now developed into the contemporary standard that both concepts are social constructions, following research that shows how chromosomes don’t necessarily match the expectation of reproductive organs as well as science’s acknowledgement of intersex persons. Scott states in 1986 that masculinity and femininity are empty and at the same time overflowing categories: they are empty since the words ’man’ and ’woman’ have no ultimate meaning; they are at the same time overflowing, because while they appear to be fixed, they still contain alternative, suppressed or denied definitions. Scott’s core definition contains two parts:

"[G]ender is a constitutive element of social relationships based on perceived differences between the sexes, and gender is a primary way of signifying relationships of power." (Scott, p. 1067)

As Scott constructs gender as a constitute of social relationships, they define gender as subjective identity, social organizations and institutions, as culturally available symbols which evoke multiple representations, and as normative concepts that fortify the meaning of these symbols. Scott describes the gendered "coding" of specific terms such that a society recognizes them as gendered terms. They exemplify this notion with the differing depiction of workers during middle-class reformations in France. Depending on the goal, the working class was either depicted as feminine or masculine, according to Scott. When the working class was supposed to be seen as weak, promotional images depicted male workers with feminine traits, such as subordination and weakness. Meanwhile when wanting to depict the working class as as strong rendered them as productive workforce and protective of their family, societally masculine traits. This difference in perception of a class in society based on their connotation with gender is an interesting manifestation of de Beauvoir’s hierarchy of gender, where the feminine is made inferior and seen as weak, while masculinity is inherently strong and capable. It is particularly interesting because it shows the additional hierarchical class oppression that is fueled by using the gendered hierarchy as a depiction. In the multifaceted hierarchy of societies, gender is one key factor in determining dominance and subordination.
